A floorball question, NHL evidence · 2010-2020

One goal down, two minutes left. When do you pull the goalie?

On the bench you get a few seconds to decide. To compute it instead, you first need a function that answers what one shot is worth, and then you have to watch how that function answers back when its inputs move. This page is that function: physics supplies one half of it, ten NHL seasons supply the other.

01The question

Can that decision be computed?

I have played floorball for nine years. The situation is always the same one: down a goal, roughly two minutes left, the goalie comes off and a sixth attacker goes on.

Everyone on the bench understands the trade. What nobody can tell you is when. Pull thirty seconds earlier and you buy a whole extra shift of pressure (and thirty more seconds of an open net) and the goal that ends it usually starts with a turnover at your own blue line.

The timing is a judgement call. This page is an attempt to turn it into a calculation, and to find where that attempt breaks.

02Shot quality

What is one shot worth?

To compute it, you first need to know how much the scoring probability changes when the goalie leaves. "Shots on goal" cannot answer that: a point shot from the blue line and a rebound from five feet out both count as one shot, yet their scoring probabilities differ by an order of magnitude.

So the first step is not the decision. It is turning "what is one shot worth" into a number, expected goals (xG): the probability that a shot taken under these conditions should score.

What follows uses public NHL data: 956,147 shots and 59,644 goals across the 2010-2020 seasons, a league goal rate of 6.24%. The heatmap and the calculator show the same thing from two directions: location decides almost everything.

03First principles

Once it leaves the stick, how does the puck fly?

Before fitting any statistical model, one part needs no data at all: how the puck flies once it leaves the stick can be derived from first principles. Below, a fourth-order Runge-Kutta integration of the puck’s equations of motion (mass 0.170 kg, diameter 0.0762 m, drag coefficient 0.90) compares trajectories with and without drag. I cross-checked my implementation against SciPy’s RK45; the relative difference in range was 0.0000%.

The second physical question is spin. Below a critical spin rate the puck tumbles and its landing point stops being controllable. One thing must be stated plainly here: the moments of inertia are computed exactly from the puck’s real geometry, but the aerodynamic overturning-moment coefficient has no published puck-specific measurement. I used a plausible range from disc-aerodynamics literature, so what follows is a curve across that range. A single number would claim more than I know. The boundary here is modelled; confirming it would take an experiment.

04Fitted models

The model says 12%. Do twelve of them go in?

Physics can tell you how the puck flies. It cannot tell you whether the goalie saves it. The rest has to be learned from data. I trained five models on the 956,147 shots: XGBoost, a deep MLP, an LSTM, a Transformer, and an Ensemble stacking them. The held-out test set is the 200,820 shots of the 2018-19 and 2019-20 seasons.

The Ensemble has the highest AUC (0.7705) and the worst calibration (10-bin weighted ECE 0.01138). The Transformer’s AUC is 0.7639 and its calibration is the best (ECE 0.00495).

That distinction decides which model I can use. AUC measures ranking, whether dangerous shots are ordered ahead of harmless ones. But the pull-goalie calculation does not need a ranking, it needs the probability itself: if the model says 12%, then twelve of every hundred such shots should go in. So the decision analysis runs on calibrated output. Having the highest AUC does not win the argument here.

05The decision

So, at which second do you pull?

Replacing the goalie with an attacker raises both teams’ scoring rates at once. Modelling each side’s goals as a Poisson arrival process gives the moment, for a given deficit and time remaining, at which pulling maximises the probability of tying or winning.

The simulator does not produce one universal "optimal moment". It depends on the attack and concession rates you put in, and moving those moves the optimum. That sensitivity is itself part of the result: this decision depends strongly on how evenly the two teams are matched.

The Poisson model is a statistical approximation that assumes events arrive independently. Player fatigue, tactical execution and goalie positioning (which Kayley ranks as the single most important factor) cannot be recovered from the available NHL data, so none of them are in the model.

06Where it fails

Where does this answer stop holding?

Nine years of floorball experience supplied the research questions; public NHL data supplied the quantitative case study. This project does not transfer NHL estimates directly to floorball.

What the models learned is association. Causation would need an intervention this data does not contain. The sensitivity ranking measures how much the fitted model’s output moves when an input changes, not how much the true scoring probability would move if the condition changed.

The gyroscopic stability boundary rests on an assumed range for an aerodynamic coefficient. Nobody has measured that coefficient for a puck.

Calibration was evaluated on an NHL test set. A different league or a different era would need recalibrating.

2. Physics Modeling: Dimensional Analysis & Key Dimensionless Parameters

Before building the ballistics and gyroscopic models, we use dimensional analysis to identify the dimensionless groups that govern system behavior. The core insight: physical laws are independent of unit choice, any system reducible to dimensionless form has behavior determined entirely by a few dimensionless ratios.

Drag Parameter in Ballistics

Puck flight involves 5 quantities: initial speed v₀ [m/s], mass m [kg], cross-section A [m²], air density ρ [kg/m³], gravitational acceleration g [m/s²]. By the Buckingham Pi theorem, 5 quantities minus 3 fundamental dimensions (M, L, T) yields 2 independent dimensionless numbers:

Drag parameter β = ρ·Cd·A·L / (2m)
β measures drag strength relative to inertia. β → 0 reduces to a parabola (introductory physics); β ≫ 1 means drag dominates. For NHL pucks, β ≈ 0.04-0.08, "drag is non-negligible but not dominant," which is precisely why numerical integration is required.
Froude number Fr = v₀² / (g·L)
Fr measures inertia vs gravity. NHL shots have Fr ≈ 30-200, confirming inertia-dominated flight, the puck doesn't arc like a basketball, but low-angle shots still show measurable drop.

Gyroscopic Stability Factor Sg

Sg is itself dimensionless: the ratio of spin angular momentum (resisting tumble) to aerodynamic overturning torque (trying to flip the puck). Under this illustrative model, Sg > 1 marks the assumed stable regime and Sg < 1 the assumed tumbling regime; the boundary is a threshold this model produces. No experiment has established it for real pucks.

The charts show the regime implied by the current assumptions. In the 0.01-0.25 range, quadratic drag is non-negligible, so this project uses numerical integration for displayed trajectories. RK4 is an accurate way to do that integration, and several other methods would serve equally well. Fr remains above one across the plotted examples, indicating inertia is large relative to gravity.

3. Model Architecture

Four architectures are designed to capture different information layers, and diversity is the prerequisite for effective ensembling.

XGBoost
AUC 0.764 · Baseline
Gradient boosted trees are a reliable benchmark for structured tabular data. The 400-tree baseline (max_depth=5, lr=0.05) intentionally does not use scale_pos_weight, preserving the raw probability scale. TreeSHAP supports direct feature-attribution analysis.
Deep MLP
AUC 0.767 · Best Non-Sequence Model
3-layer fully connected network (512→256→128, BatchNorm+Dropout=0.3). Learns high-order feature interactions: "close distance + front-on + tip-in" is far more dangerous than the sum of each factor alone.
LSTM
AUC 0.762 · Sequence Context
Captures context from the 8 events preceding each shot (event type + coordinates + time delta). Vectorized with pandas groupby+shift: sequence construction in 6 sec vs 4 hours with Python loops.
Transformer
AUC 0.7639 · ECE 0.00495
A 4-layer Transformer encoder processes the same event window used by the LSTM. Its held-out AUC is 0.7639 and its 10-bin weighted ECE is 0.00495. Both numbers come from the NHL test set. Floorball is a separate question this project does not answer.
Ensemble
AUC 0.771 · Stacked Model
A Logistic Regression stacking blender combines calibrated model predictions. On the 2018-19 and 2019-20 held-out seasons, Ensemble AUC is 0.7705 and ECE is 0.01138. Evaluation code and public JSON share one canonical exporter.

4. Evaluation Metrics

These two definitions follow scripts/analysis_metrics.py line for line. Where the general textbook form differs, the code is what produced the numbers below. Bins are K=10 uniform-width bins on [0,1]; nk is the count in bin k, N the total, ȳk the observed goal rate in that bin, p̄k the mean predicted probability, and ȳ the overall goal rate.

This is the "10-bin weighted ECE" reported above: the gap between predicted probability and observed frequency, weighted by how many shots fall in each bin. It measures calibration and is independent of discrimination (AUC) — which is exactly why the Ensemble has the highest AUC and the worst ECE, and the Transformer the reverse.

The three-term decomposition of the Brier score: reliability (lower is better), resolution (higher is better), and uncertainty (which belongs to the data itself). Note that the Brier score reported here is computed directly from the predictions (sklearn.metrics.brier_score_loss), while the three components are binned estimators; the identity BS = REL − RES + UNC holds exactly for the binned estimator, and the two are computed separately.

6. Key Findings

Finding 1: The High-Danger Triangle

The area within 20 ft of the net, within 45° angle (the "High Danger Zone") has an average xG of 0.143 and goal rate of 14.3%—more than twice the league average. This triangle is the strategic ground that defenses must protect and offenses must penetrate.

Finding 2: Power Play Multiplier

High-danger PP avg xG = 0.374, vs. EV 0.172, a 2.2× multiplier. PP teams have more time, space, and open shooting lanes. Optimal PP strategy: organise to get inside the slot. Perimeter shots waste the advantage.

Finding 3: Shot Type Matters 3×

Tip-Ins and Deflections (~23% goal rate) vs Slap Shots (~8%). Counter-intuitive: the "most powerful" shot type is the easiest to save because goalies read the wind-up. Unpredictability beats raw power.

Finding 4: Pull Goalie Optimal Window

Poisson model: when trailing by 1, the expected-value crossover occurs around 60-90 seconds remaining. Pulling earlier risks too many empty-net goals; waiting too long loses the offensive upside.

7. Limitations

NHL play-by-play data records what happened but not where players were. Two critical missing factors:

  • Goalie positioning: Whether the goalie cuts angles is the single biggest determinant of shot difficulty. Requires NHL Player Tracking Data or video-based YOLOv8 detection.
  • Defensive screens: Blocked-shot data records only the outcome; where the defender actually stood is absent.

This model is limited to the variables present in public play-by-play data. More complete analysis would require tracking or video data for goalie and defender positions.
