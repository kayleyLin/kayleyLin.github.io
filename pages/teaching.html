Stepping-Stones / Teaching

Algorithm teaching · Shanghai · four years

Where an analogy stops working, and for whom

For four years I taught algorithm concepts (path-planning, sorting, dynamic programming) to migrant-worker kids in Shanghai, ages eleven to thirteen, in small in-person groups. None of them had touched code before. The method was simple and slow: compress each concept into an analogy that an eleven-year-old could hold, then watch exactly where it snapped. Every snap became the next version. The log of those revisions is the actual work.

Pressure I applied

Take the abstract. Compress it until a sixth-grader can hold it.

I started with textbook definitions, "a path-finding algorithm computes the minimum-cost route between nodes in a weighted graph." Immediate, uniform confusion. Not because the students were slow. Because the sentence had no foothold in anything they already knew.

The constraint I set myself: every concept had to enter through something already in the room, a queue at the cafeteria, a price tag at the market, a door that sticks. Compression under that constraint is the same as any other pressure test. You push the concept into a smaller container until something breaks.

Where the system failed

Every analogy fails for some kid, at some point. That point is data.

The delivery-route analogy for path-planning worked until weighted edges appeared. The moment I introduced "some roads take longer than others," students stopped being able to map "cost" onto their mental model of a delivery route, because in their experience, a delivery uncle doesn't think in units of cost, he thinks in landmarks and turns.

That failure was not a mistake in the analogy. It was a precise measurement: the analogy held through unweighted graphs and broke at the first edge weight. Which meant the next version had to do one specific thing, give "cost" a concrete form that an eleven-year-old already navigates every day.

What I changed

School-context anchors. Doors that stick.

Version three of the path-planning analogy dropped the delivery uncle entirely. It became: "which classroom doors do you go through to reach the bathroom fastest, when some doors are stuck?" Cost became stuckness, something with a body-level meaning. It held through weighted edges.

The principle that emerged: analogies break at the exact point where an abstract property (cost, order, subproblem overlap) has no concrete counterpart in the student's existing world. Finding that point and replacing it with something real is the only revision that works. Patching the surface of the analogy doesn't.

v1
"Path-planning is finding the shortest route between two cities."
Failed immediately, students had no map, no GPS, no mental model of cities as a graph. The abstraction had nowhere to land.
v2
"Path-planning is your delivery uncle's daily route."
Worked better, students could picture the route, the sequence of stops. Broke as soon as weighted edges appeared: students had no model for "cost" on a route their uncle navigated by landmarks; he has never once thought about it in minutes or metres.
v3
"Path-planning is deciding which classroom doors to go through to reach the bathroom fastest, when some doors are stuck."
Concrete. School-context. Weighted edges become stuck doors, cost has a physical meaning students navigate daily. Held through the full algorithm.
